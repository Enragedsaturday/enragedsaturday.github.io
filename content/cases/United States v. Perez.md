# United States v. Perez

## Background
Gilbert Perez was convicted of a federal drug offense in the District of Maine after the district court denied his motion to suppress the fruits of a warrantless search of his backpack, which officers searched incident to his arrest. The district court rested its ruling on *United States v. Eatherton*, a First Circuit decision upholding a similar warrantless search of a bag within an arrestee's reach under the search-incident-to-arrest exception. On appeal, Perez argued that intervening Supreme Court decisions — *[[United States v. Chadwick|Chadwick]]* and *[[Arizona v. Gant]]* — had stripped *Eatherton* of its controlling force.

## Issue
Whether the First Circuit's search-incident-to-arrest rule permitting the warrantless search of a bag the arrestee carried or wore at the time of arrest, as applied in *Eatherton*, survives the Supreme Court's decisions in *[[United States v. Chadwick|Chadwick]]* and *[[Arizona v. Gant|Gant]]*.

## Rule
The search-incident-to-arrest exception allows officers, without a warrant, to search the arrestee's person and personal property he carries or wears at the time of arrest, including a container such as a bag; *Eatherton* applied that rule to a bag carried at arrest, and the panel held it remains binding after *[[United States v. Chadwick|Chadwick]]* and *[[Arizona v. Gant|Gant]]*: "Because we conclude that *Eatherton* controls here, we need not evaluate the search of Perez's backpack under *Maldonaldo-Espinosa*." — 89 F.4th 247, slip op. at 17. ^pin-op17

## Application
Perez's core contention was that *[[United States v. Chadwick|Chadwick]]* (which held the warrantless search of an arrestee's double-locked footlocker unlawful) and *[[Arizona v. Gant|Gant]]* (which cabined vehicle [[Search Incident to Arrest|searches incident to arrest]] to circumstances where the arrestee can access the passenger compartment or the vehicle may contain evidence of the offense) had eroded *Eatherton*'s allowance for a warrantless search of a bag carried or worn at arrest. The court disagreed: *[[Arizona v. Gant|Gant]]* addressed only the distinct automobile context and said nothing about containers an arrestee carries at the time of arrest, and *[[United States v. Chadwick|Chadwick]]* concerned a heavy, double-locked footlocker rather than property carried or worn on the person. If anything, *[[Riley v. California|Riley]]* cut against Perez: it described *[[Arizona v. Gant|Gant]]* as an automobile case and merely excluded a cell phone's digital contents from the search-incident rule. Neither intervening precedent displaced *Eatherton*, which controlled; the panel therefore did not reach the government's alternative *Maldonaldo-Espinosa* ground, and the warrantless search of the backpack Perez was wearing at his arrest was lawful.

## Conclusion
**Affirmed.** Chief Judge Barron wrote for the panel (Barron, C.J., Howard, and Montecalvo, JJ.).
