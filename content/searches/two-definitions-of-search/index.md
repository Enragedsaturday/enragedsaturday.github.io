# Two Definitions of Search — Trespass & Privacy

Before any warrant or exception is in play, every Fourth Amendment problem starts with one threshold question: did the government do something the Amendment even counts as a **"search"**? There are **two independent answers**, and satisfying **either** one is enough. This sub-umbrella holds the two, with a page developing each.

- [[Trespass]] — the property answer. It is a search when officers **physically intrude** on a constitutionally protected area (a person, house, paper, or effect) **to obtain information**. This is the older, common-law test, dormant for decades and revived by *[[United States v. Jones]]*.
- [[Reasonable Expectation of Privacy]] — the privacy answer. It is a search when the government **invades a [[Reasonable Expectation of Privacy|reasonable expectation of privacy]]**, one the person kept and one society accepts as reasonable, even with no physical entry at all. This is the modern *Katz* test.

**How the two fit together.** The tests run **in parallel, not in sequence**: the privacy test was "added to, not substituted for" the trespass test, so you may find a search under one without ever reaching the other. A trespass to gather information is a search even where a privacy analysis would be contested, and a privacy invasion is a search even with no physical trespass. If **neither** answer is met, there is no "search," and the warrant-and-reasonableness machinery never engages.

A parallel branch of the same threshold is the **seizure of property**, which turns on meaningful interference with possession rather than on privacy or trespass; it is introduced with the property line on [[Trespass]].
