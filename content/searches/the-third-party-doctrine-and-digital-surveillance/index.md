# The Third-Party Doctrine and Digital Surveillance

This sub-umbrella collects the Fourth Amendment doctrine that governs **information held by, or generated through, third parties** — and the digital-age technologies that test it. The organizing rule is the **third-party doctrine**: information a person voluntarily conveys to an intermediary generally loses Fourth Amendment protection. Its modern limit is *[[Carpenter v. United States|Carpenter]]*, which pulled the comprehensive, automatically generated record of a person's movements back inside the Amendment. Everything below is either that core rule or a specific technology that lives on the line *[[Carpenter v. United States|Carpenter]]* drew.

- [[Third-Party Doctrine & CSLI]] — the **anchor**. The *[[Smith v. Maryland|Smith]]*/*[[United States v. Miller|Miller]]* third-party rule and the *[[Carpenter v. United States|Carpenter]]* CSLI carve-out; the two-track test every other page applies. Start here.
- [[Cell-Site Simulators]] — StingRay / IMSI-catcher deployment: a device that impersonates a tower to locate a phone, often inside a home. No SCOTUS rule; warrant-requiring by analogy and by policy.
- [[Reverse-Keyword and Geofence Warrants]] — reverse-location and reverse-search demands. *[[Chatrie v. United States|Chatrie]]* settled that geofence acquisition is a search; the [[Particularity|particularity]] of the warrants is the open frontier.
- [[Real-Time Tracking]] — beepers, GPS, and real-time CSLI: *[[United States v. Knotts|Knotts]]*/*[[United States v. Karo|Karo]]*/*[[United States v. Jones|Jones]]* and the reserved real-time-location question.
- [[Investigative Genetic Genealogy]] — matching crime-scene DNA against consumer genealogy databases; no controlling precedent, taught as unsettled.

**Alongside, not below.** [[Electronic Surveillance and Title III]] is the sibling that governs interception of communication **contents** (the *[[Olmstead v. United States|Olmstead]]*/*[[Katz v. United States|Katz]]*/*[[Berger v. New York|Berger]]* line and the Title III statutory wiretap regime), as distinct from the business-records and location data this family otherwise covers.

**How the pieces fit.** The recurring question is which of two tracks a new form of data sits on: conventional information voluntarily conveyed to a third party (no search, no warrant) or a comprehensive, automatically generated digital record (a search requiring a warrant). Work from the [[Third-Party Doctrine & CSLI|anchor rule]] outward to the specific tool, and remember the threshold theories that sit beneath all of it — the [[Reasonable Expectation of Privacy|privacy]] and [[Trespass|trespass]] definitions of a search.
